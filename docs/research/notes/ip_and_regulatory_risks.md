## 1. What copyright protects in games: idea vs expression, and the key US cases (plus 2020–2026 cases)

### Takeaway
US courts consistently hold that game rules and mechanics (the "idea") are free to copy. What they protect is the specific expression: art, characters, audio, text, code, and in some cases the overall visual "look and feel" and the selection and arrangement of expressive elements. Tetris v. Xio (2012) and Spry Fox v. 6waves (2012) show that a near-identical *presentation* of the same mechanics loses, even with re-skinned art. DaVinci v. ZiKo (2016) and Data East v. Epyx (1988) show that copying the *rules* under a different theme and look wins. Most 2020–2026 mobile clone disputes settled. The clearest merits wins came in China (Pokémon, about US$15M in 2024) and Korea (Nexon v. Ironmace, where the copyright claim failed but the trade-secret claim succeeded, finalised April 2026).

### Cited Findings

**Tetris Holding v. Xio Interactive (D.N.J. 2012; 863 F. Supp. 2d 394)**
- Xio released *Mino*, a mobile game built on Tetris gameplay. The case confirmed that a game's look and feel can be protected under copyright. — [Wikipedia: Tetris Holding v. Xio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetris_Holding,_LLC_v._Xio_Interactive,_Inc.)
- Xio's main defence was merger and scènes à faire. The court rejected it, reasoning that Xio's broad reading of those doctrines "would create an exception that would likely swallow any protection a game could have." It also found that there are countless ways to express a falling-block puzzle game (e.g., Dr. Mario), so idea and expression had not merged. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetris_Holding,_LLC_v._Xio_Interactive,_Inc.); [Lizerbram Law](https://www.lizerbramlaw.com/blog/tetris-case-video-game-copyright/)
- Tetris obtained a permanent injunction against Xio in early 2013. — [IPWatchdog, 12 Feb 2013](https://ipwatchdog.com/2013/02/12/tetris-gets-permanent-injunction-against-xio/)
- Commentators read Tetris together with later cases as meaning US protection for games "may only be skin deep". Protection attaches to the visual and audiovisual expression rather than the rules. — [Mondaq / FKKS: "the practical limit to US copyright protection may only be skin deep"](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/gaming/591564/when-it-comes-to-games-the-practical-limit-to-united-states-copyright-protection-may-only-be-skin-deep)
- *Background knowledge, not verified this session:* the opinion identified copied expressive elements including the 20x10 playfield, the seven tetrimino shapes and their colours, the "ghost" piece, garbage lines and next-piece display. It also granted summary judgment on a trade dress claim covering the look of the pieces and board. Treat this as unconfirmed until the opinion is checked.

**Spry Fox v. Lolapps/6waves (W.D. Wash. 2012), Triple Town vs Yeti Town**
- Spry Fox alleged that 6waves' *Yeti Town* was "little more than a barely-disguised clone" of *Triple Town*. The court refused to give Spry Fox a monopoly on the underlying idea. It nevertheless found that the "objectively similar expression" in Yeti Town was plausibly alleged and denied the motion to dismiss. — [MCV/Develop](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/court-denies-motion-to-dismiss-spry-fox-vs-6waves)
- Different art did not defeat the claim. The court found Yeti Town plausibly appropriated expressive elements including the *hierarchy of objects* and the presence of a wild creature that tries to foil the player's progress. — [MCV/Develop](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/court-denies-motion-to-dismiss-spry-fox-vs-6waves); [Wikipedia: Spry Fox v. Lolapps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spry_Fox,_LLC_v._Lolapps,_Inc.)
- The case settled out of court in October 2012. Spry Fox took ownership of the Yeti Town IP, and Yeti Town was pulled from sale. — [PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/spry-foxs-triple-town-triumph-sees-studio-pick-up-yeti-town-ip); [AOL/Joystiq, 13 Oct 2012](https://www.aol.com/2012-10-13-spry-fox-6waves-lawsuit-settled.html)

**DaVinci Editrice v. ZiKo Games (S.D. Tex., No. 4:13-cv-03415, decided April 2016)**
- DaVinci (publisher of the card game *Bang!*) sued Yoka Games and its US distributor ZiKo over *Legends of the Three Kingdoms*. The parties agreed the two games have nearly identical rules. — [Justia: Document 73 (S.D. Tex. 2016)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/texas/txsdce/4:2013cv03415/1134359/73/); [MCV: "Game mechanics cannot be protected by copyright"](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/game-mechanics-cannot-be-protected-by-copyright/)
- The court denied DaVinci's summary judgment motion and granted the defendants'. It held that the characters, roles and interactions were not substantially similar, and that the similar parts were not expressive. The games differ visually: *Bang!* uses Wild West imagery, while *Legends of the Three Kingdoms* uses Chinese-history theming. — [Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/texas/txsdce/4:2013cv03415/1134359/73/); [FKKS: How courts view copyright protection for video games](https://fkks.com/news/how-courts-view-copyright-protection-for-video-games)
- Commentators disagree on how broadly to read the case. Some treat it as a general rule that mechanics are uncopyrightable. Others tie it to its facts: different theme and art, and no fixed progression of events. — [Nintendo Life, June 2016](https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2016/06/texas_court_ruling_states_that_game_mechanics_cant_be_protected_by_copyright); [TechRaptor](https://techraptor.net/content/game-mechanics-arent-protected-copyright-according-texas-court-ruling)

**Data East USA v. Epyx (9th Cir. 1988; 862 F.2d 204)**
- Data East v. Epyx is cited for the proposition that a clone with similar ideas and principles to another game is permissible, because copyright protects only the specific expression of an idea. — [Medium: "Clones Wars: Video Game Litigation Illustrated"](https://medium.com/patents-technology-law/clones-wars-video-game-litigation-illustrated-36682abb4d68)
- *Background knowledge, not verified this session:* the case was *Karate Champ* vs *World Karate Championship*. The Ninth Circuit reversed a finding of infringement because the shared elements (karate moves, scoring, referee, two-fighter format) flowed from the sport itself and were scènes à faire or unprotectable ideas.

**Scènes à faire generally**
- Courts treat common tropes that define a genre as unprotectable stock elements. — [Wikipedia: Tetris v. Xio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetris_Holding,_LLC_v._Xio_Interactive,_Inc.); [Lizerbram Law](https://www.lizerbramlaw.com/blog/tetris-case-video-game-copyright/)

**Newer cases, 2020–2026 (mostly settlements)**
- **Krafton v. Garena, Apple, Google, YouTube (C.D. Cal., filed 10 Jan 2022).** Krafton alleged that *Free Fire* and *Free Fire Max* copied copyrighted aspects of *PUBG: Battlegrounds*, including game structure, in-game items, equipment and locations. It also sued Apple and Google for continuing to distribute the games after being notified. Krafton had earlier settled a Singapore case with Garena in 2017. — [TechCrunch, 13 Jan 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/01/13/pubg-mobile-maker-krafton-sues-apple-google-and-rival-game-developer-garena-over-clones/); [PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/asia/news/77889/krafton-sues-garena-apple-google-over-free-fire-copyright-infringement/). The final outcome of the 2022 US suit was **not found** (see Gaps).
- **The Pokémon Company v. *Pocket Monster: Remake* developers (Shenzhen court, China).** The court ruled for Pokémon in September 2024 and awarded about US$15M. A settlement was finalised in December 2024 and announced on 19 Feb 2025. — [Hitmarker](https://hitmarker.net/news/the-pokemon-company-secures-emphatic-victory-in-copyright-infringement-case-against-chinese-clone-2922753); [OpenCritic](https://opencritic.com/news/12718/after-a-decade-the-pok-mon-company-has-finally-gotten-an-apology-and-15-million-in-damages-for-mobile-pokemon-clone)
- **Nexon v. Ironmace (*Dark and Darker*, Korea).** On 13 Feb 2025 the Seoul Central District Court held there was **no copyright infringement** but found trade-secret misappropriation and awarded KRW 8.5bn. On appeal the award was cut to about KRW 5.7bn. The Supreme Court dismissed both sides' appeals on 30 April 2026, finalising the decision. The courts treated information about an unreleased project ("P3") as a trade secret, and the Supreme Court noted the games were in different sub-genres (battle royale vs extraction). — [PC Gamer, Feb 2025](https://www.pcgamer.com/gaming-industry/dark-and-darker-developer-did-not-commit-copyright-infringement-court-rules-but-has-to-pay-nexon-nearly-usd6-million-anyway); [Inven Global](https://www.invenglobal.com/articles/21463/nexon-wins-partial-supreme-court-victory-against-ironmace-in-trade-secret-misappropriation-case); [Asia Economy, 13 Feb 2025](https://www.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2025021316103509710)
- **Sony v. Tencent (*Light of Motiram* vs *Horizon*, N.D./C.D. Cal.).** Filed July 2025, alleging a "slavish clone". It was dismissed with prejudice after a settlement, with court documents dated 17 Dec 2025, and the game was delisted. There was no merits ruling. — [Insider Gaming](https://insider-gaming.com/horizon-clone-lawsuit-settled-with-light-of-motiram-delisted/); [ScreenHub](https://www.screenhub.com.au/news/games/light-of-motiram-sony-tencent-lawsuit-2689447/)
- **Square Enix v. HK Ten Tree (*Metal Storm*, W.D. Wash.).** Concerned reuse of assets from a cancelled joint project. Settled in May 2025. — [Gaming Amigos](https://www.gamingamigos.com/post/square-enix-settles-lawsuit-ten-tree)
- **Ilyon Dynamics v. Kings Fortune (N.D. Cal.).** The court denied a motion to dismiss copyright and trademark claims over a mobile game. The date and final outcome are unclear. — [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/ip-law/case-copyrights-trademarks-pleadings-n-d-cal-2)
- **Avia Games v. Oakever Games.** A second copyright clone suit against Oakever (*Vita Mahjong* etc.), filed December 2025. No outcome found. — [MobileGamer.biz](https://mobilegamer.biz/vita-mahjong-amaze-go-and-meowdoku-maker-oakever-games-fights-cloning-accusations/)

**Patents: a separate risk that does cover mechanics**
- A Texas jury found Supercell's games willfully infringed GREE patents, covering among other things methods for presenting downloadable games and in-game funds, and awarded at least US$8.5M (Sept 2020). — [DisputeSoft](https://www.disputesoft.com/video-game-developer-awarded-8-5-million-in-patent-dispute-against-clash-of-clans-creator/); [Law.com, 22 Sept 2020](https://law.com/therecorder/2020/09/22/clash-of-video-gamers-results-in-8-5-million-jury-verdict-403-54843)
- A separate GREE patent suit naming *Clash Royale* survived a motion to dismiss. — [Patent Arcade](https://www.patentarcade.com/tag/clash-royale)
- A WIPR headline refers to a "$92m" patent ruling against the Clash of Clans maker. The details were not verified. — [World IP Review](https://www.worldipreview.com/patent/clash-of-clans-maker-owes-92m-says-patent-ruling-21334)
- Commentary on DaVinci noted that mechanics are better protected by patent law than by copyright. — [MCV](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/game-mechanics-cannot-be-protected-by-copyright/)

### Inferences
- **Protectable vs not, in summary:**
  - *Not protected by copyright:* rules, mechanics, systems and genre conventions. Examples are an elixir-style regenerating resource, card cycling, lanes, towers, jungle camps, a 5v5 or 1v1 format, a "deploy units that auto-path down lanes" loop, rarity tiers, and ladder/trophy progression in the abstract.
  - *Protected:* code; character and unit art and animation; character names and personalities as fleshed out; audio and music; written text; icons; specific UI art. Also the specific *selection, arrangement and presentation* of elements. Tetris (visual look) and Spry Fox (hierarchy of objects plus antagonist creature) show this can reach structural choices when copied wholesale.
  - *Trademark / trade dress:* game titles, logos, distinctive icons, possibly a distinctive overall look (Tetris trade dress is unconfirmed, see above).
  - *Patents:* specific technical or game-method implementations (GREE v. Supercell), which bite even when everything is original-looking.
  - *Trade secrets:* unreleased design documents and internal data (Nexon v. Ironmace).
- A hybrid that combines Clash Royale-style real-time card and elixir deployment with a MLBB-style 3-lane map is, at the level of mechanics, very unlikely to infringe copyright in either game. The risk lies in how closely the *presentation* tracks either source: map art and landmark placement, unit and hero designs, card frames, UI layout, names, and numbers.
- Settlements dominate recent clone fights. For a small studio, the practical risk is legal cost, takedowns and forced delisting rather than final judgments. Large rights-holders (Tencent/Supercell, ByteDance/Moonton) have the resources to litigate.

### Gaps
- Primary opinions (Tetris 863 F. Supp. 2d 394; Data East 862 F.2d 204; Spry Fox order; DaVinci Doc. 73) were not opened because fetching was blocked. Specific holdings marked "background knowledge" need checking.
- Status and outcome of Krafton v. Garena/Apple/Google (2022 US suit): no source found on settlement or judgment.
- No US appellate decision from 2020–2026 squarely on mobile-game mechanics vs expression was found.

## 2. MOBA-specific precedents: Riot/Tencent vs Moonton (Mobile Legends) and Blizzard/Valve (DOTA)

### Takeaway
Riot never obtained a US merits ruling against Moonton. Both US suits (2017 and 2022) were dismissed on forum grounds, with the dispute pushed to China. In China, Tencent obtained about RMB 19.4M (about US$2.9M) in 2018, though reporting conflicts on whether this was against Moonton's founder personally and on what basis. The whole dispute ended in a confidential global settlement in April 2024. Earlier, Moonton's original *Mobile Legends: 5v5 MOBA* was pulled from Google Play after a Riot complaint and relaunched as *Mobile Legends: Bang Bang*. The DOTA dispute shows that a genre-defining name can be owned as a trademark.

### Cited Findings

**Riot/Tencent vs Moonton**
- **Platform takedown (2016/2017).** After Riot complained to Google, Moonton pulled *Mobile Legends: 5v5 MOBA* from Google Play and re-released it as *Mobile Legends: Bang Bang*. Sources disagree on whether this happened in late 2016 or 2017. — [Sportskeeda](https://sportskeeda.com/esports/mobile-legends-tracing-origin-india-s-banned-game); [Wikipedia: Mobile Legends: Bang Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_Legends:_Bang_Bang); [Mein-MMO](https://mein-mmo.de/en/developers-have-allegedly-been-copying-league-of-legends-boldly-and-successfully-for-years-but-riot-has-no-desire-to-keep-watching-this,823380)
- **2017 US suit (C.D. Cal.).** Riot sued Moonton over alleged copying of League of Legends elements. The suit also covered Moonton's *Magic Rush: Heroes* and the 5v5 MOBA title. — [PC Gamer, 2017](https://www.pcgamer.com/riot-sues-mobile-legends-bang-bang-maker-for-reasons-you-can-probably-guess); [2017 complaint (DocumentCloud)](https://assets.documentcloud.org/documents/3896724/Https-Ecf-Cacd-Uscourts-Gov-doc1-031126177996.pdf); [OneEsports](https://www.oneesports.gg/mobile-legends/moonton-games-riot-games-lawsuit/). - **What was alleged.** Available summaries describe copying of in-game elements, gameplay, character designs and abilities. **No search result confirmed a specific "Summoner's Rift map layout" allegation**, although the complaint PDF above would be the place to check. — [OneEsports](https://www.oneesports.gg/mobile-legends/moonton-games-riot-games-lawsuit/); [Dugout PH](https://www.dugout.ph/2024/04/riot-moonton-reach-global-settlement.html)
- **China ruling (about July 2018).** Reports conflict:
  - DotEsports says a Chinese ruling ordered Moonton CEO Xu Zhenhua to pay about RMB 19.4M (about US$2.9M). — [DotEsports](https://dotesports.com/league-of-legends/news/source-riot-games-parent-tencent-wins-lawsuit-mobile-legends-31079)
  - Game Developer describes Tencent being owed about US$2.9M in a suit against the Mobile Legends developer. — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/tencent-awarded-2-9m-in-case-against-dev-of-alleged-i-league-of-legends-i-clone)

  - OneEsports simply says Tencent "won and Moonton paid US$2.9 million." — [OneEsports](https://www.oneesports.gg/mobile-legends/moonton-games-riot-games-lawsuit/)
- **Moonton's counter-suit (defamation).** In 2019 Moonton sued Tencent for commercial defamation over Tencent's public claims about winning the case. A Shanghai court ordered about RMB 220,000 in damages, reported October 2022. Most outlets name Tencent as the payer; one names Riot. — [Spin.ph, 13 Oct 2022](https://www.spin.ph/esports/bytedance-s-moonton-games-sued-tencent-for-commercial-slander-receives-220-000-yuan-a1374-20221013); [Esports.gg](https://cache.esports.gg/news/gaming/tencent-vs-moonton-lawsuit)
- **2022 US suit.** After ByteDance acquired Moonton, Riot sued again in 2022. It alleged that MLBB closely resembled *League of Legends* and had been updated to copy "extensive expressive elements" from *Wild Rift*. — [WIPR](https://www.worldipreview.com/copyright/riot-sues-bytedance-unit-over-league-of-legends-clone-22338); [Unbox PH](https://unbox.ph/news/riot-games-sues-moonton-again-over-mobile-legends/amp/)
- **2022 dismissal.** In November 2022 a Los Angeles federal judge dismissed the case on forum non conveniens grounds, saying it belonged in China. - **Global settlement (announced about 1–2 April 2024).** Moonton announced a global settlement of the IP disputes, and Riot withdrew the related lawsuits. The terms were undisclosed and reportedly confidential. — [Esports Legal News, 2 Apr 2024](https://esportslegal.news/2024/04/02/moonton-and-riot-games-settlement-ip-dispute/); [Esports.net](https://www.esports.net/news/industry/riot-games-moonton-legal-dispute/); [Techdirt, 2 Apr 2024](https://www.techdirt.com/2024/04/02/riot-games-moonton-settle-copyright-dispute-after-7-years-of-drama/)

**Blizzard v. Valve (DOTA trademark, 2012)**
- Blizzard opposed Valve's trademark application for "DOTA". On 11 May 2012 they settled. Valve got commercial rights to the DOTA mark (so *Dota 2* kept its name). Blizzard kept non-commercial use for modders and map makers and renamed its own game *Blizzard All-Stars*, which later became *Heroes of the Storm*. Terms were undisclosed. — [Engadget, 11 May 2012](https://www.engadget.com/2012-05-11-blizzard-and-valve-settle-dota-argument-blizzard-dota-is-now-bl.html); [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/news/archive/2012/05/11/valve-blizzard-reach-dota-trademark-agreement); [Liquipedia](https://liquipedia.net/dota2/Blizzard_Entertainment,_Inc._v_Valve_Corporation)

### Inferences
- The Moonton saga shows a **platform takedown** can force a rename and rework long before any court rules. This was the 2016/17 Google Play removal of the 5v5 MOBA title. For a small studio with no cash buffer, a takedown is the most realistic "enforcement event".
- MLBB survived and thrived despite obvious LoL lineage. A 3-lane map with towers, jungle, minions and objectives is therefore effectively a shared genre convention. Riot's later suits focused on *expressive* elements (heroes, abilities, designs, updates tracking Wild Rift), not on the existence of lanes.
- Forum and jurisdiction matter. Riot's US suits failed on forum grounds because Moonton was a Chinese company. A Malaysian studio could still be sued in Malaysia, or in the US if it targets US users. Forum dismissals are not a defence on the merits.
- The DOTA dispute shows that genre-identifying words can be owned. Use generic descriptors ("MOBA", "lane battler", "card battler") in store copy and avoid a rival's coined terms in the title.

### Gaps
- What exactly the 2017 complaint alleged (map, specific heroes, names) was not confirmed from the complaint text; only the PDF link was found.
- The legal basis of the 2018 Chinese ruling (copyright vs unfair competition vs a claim against Xu personally) is reported inconsistently. No primary judgment was found.
- No source was found on Moonton's trademark registrations ("Mobile Legends", "Bang Bang", or in-game objective names such as "Lord" or "Turtle"). This is unconfirmed and should be checked on MyIPO, USPTO and EUIPO.

## 3. Supercell enforcement history and trademark coverage ("Clash", "Royale", "Elixir")

### Takeaway
No copyright or trademark lawsuit by Supercell against a Clash Royale gameplay clone was found. Supercell's visible responses have been DMCA takedowns (mainly against bot and cheat sites) and, in April 2026, a "Copycats Welcome" marketing campaign that converted clone players into Clash Royale players. Supercell does hold US registrations for the word **CLASH** in game classes, so "Clash" in a title is high-risk. No evidence was found either way on "Royale" or "Elixir" registrations.

### Cited Findings
- Clash Royale clones appeared quickly. A Chinese company cloned the game within about a week of its 2016 soft launch. — [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/chinese-company-clones-supercells-clash-royale-in-just-a-week/)
- Trade press analysis found that Clash Royale clones mostly struggled commercially. Reasons given were that synchronous PvP is hard to build and balance, and that clones lack Supercell-level platform support. One commentator suggested successful followers borrow progression systems rather than directly cloning core gameplay. — [PocketGamer.biz: Are Clash Royale clones making any money?](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/are-clash-royale-clones-making-any-money); [PocketGamer.biz: Why does Clash Royale have no worthy competition?](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/why-does-clash-royale-have-no-worthy-competition)
- Supercell's DMCA notices state that it "owns all legal rights to use and enforce all copyrights" in Clash of Clans, Clash Royale, Hay Day and Boom Beach, "including use of images and icons from those games". The notices were directed at bot and cheat repositories. — [GitHub DMCA repo mirror, 2021-12-28 Supercell notice](https://git.randommeaninglesscharacters.com/github/dmca/src/branch/master/2021/12/2021-12-28-supercell.md); [2021-02-24 Supercell notice](https://git.randommeaninglesscharacters.com/github/dmca/src/branch/master/2021/02/2021-02-24-supercell.md)
- **"Copycats Welcome" (April 2026).** Created by DAVID New York for Clash Royale, the campaign let players submit evidence of progress in copycat games in exchange for Clash Royale rewards (gold, Wild Cards). The earliest coverage is dated about 11–16 April 2026, with case studies in June 2026. One promotional source claims over 2.3M players transferred progress in the first five days. That figure is unverified and comes from a promotional framing. — [Ogilvy/DAVID: Copycats Welcome](https://www.ogilvy.com/work/copycats-welcome); [Ads of Brands](https://adsofbrands.net/public/en/news/david-new-york-calls-out-copycat-games-with-new-clash-royale-campaign/9848); [InGameNews, Apr 2026](https://ingamenews.com/2026/04/clash-royale-copycats-welcome-campaign.html); [The Stable](https://www.thestable.com.au/david-new-york-clash-royale-outplays-its-copycats/)
- **Trademarks.** Supercell Oy holds a USPTO registration for **CLASH** (filed 2018) in Class 9 (software) and Class 41 (entertainment). A 2021 application covers Classes 9, 16, 25, 28 and 41, and a 2023 application covers Class 24. — [TrademarkDir: CLASH 79232107](https://trademarkdir.com/trademark/clash-79232107); [Markinton: CLASH 79382712](https://markinton.com/trademark/clash-79382712); [Markinton: CLASH 79317764](https://markinton.com/trademark/clash-79317764)
- Supercell has itself been the defendant in game-method patent cases (GREE). — [DisputeSoft](https://www.disputesoft.com/video-game-developer-awarded-8-5-million-in-patent-dispute-against-clash-of-clans-creator/)

### Inferences
- Supercell's enforcement posture toward *gameplay* clones appears to be marketing-led rather than litigation-led. Its registered rights in **CLASH** and its art and icons are what it actively asserts. A title or keywords using "Clash", or icons resembling Clash Royale's (character faces with crown motifs, card frames, pink/purple "elixir" droplet), are the most likely triggers for a store complaint.
- "Elixir" as the name of a regenerating deploy-resource is closely associated with Clash Royale. Even without confirmed registration, renaming the resource (e.g., "Flux", "Charge", "Mana-tide") and giving it a different colour and icon costs nothing and removes an easy "confusingly similar" argument.
- Because Supercell is now owned by Tencent (as is Riot), a game that imitates both Clash Royale and an LoL-lineage MOBA could draw interest from the same corporate group. This is speculative.

### Gaps
- No evidence was found of Supercell suing a gameplay clone for copyright or trademark. Absence of evidence is not proof; store-level complaints are not public.
- No evidence was found on whether "ROYALE", "CLASH ROYALE" device marks or "ELIXIR" are registered in Malaysia (MyIPO), the EU or the US for games. These should be checked directly in the MyIPO, USPTO TESS and EUIPO eSearch databases.
- No EUIPO opposition decisions by Supercell over "CLASH"-formative marks were found.

## 4. Malaysian copyright and trademark law relevant to games

### Takeaway
Malaysia's Copyright Act 1987 protects computer programs as literary works, automatically and without registration, alongside artistic works, sound recordings and films. Game mechanics as such are not addressed, and no Malaysian case law on game cloning was found. The Trademarks Act 2019 allows non-traditional marks (shapes, sounds and more) and protects well-known marks even when unregistered in Malaysia. Global brands like Clash Royale or Mobile Legends can therefore likely be enforced in Malaysia without local registration.

### Cited Findings
- Under the Copyright Act 1987, computer programs are specifically protected as literary works, and protection arises automatically on creation of a qualifying work, without registration. — [Asia IP: Malaysia IP Guide 2025](https://asiaiplaw.com/article/malaysia-ip-guide-2025); [WIPO Lex: Copyright Act 1987](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/legislation/details/7603); [Low & Partners: Copyright Act 1987](https://www.lowpartners.com/copyright-act-1987-part-1-2/)
- The Trademarks Act 2019 replaced the 1976 Act. It expanded registrable marks to non-traditional marks such as 3D shapes, sounds and scents, and gives enhanced protection to well-known marks, including those not registered in Malaysia. — [Asia IP: Malaysia IP Guide 2025](https://www.asiaiplaw.com/sector/trademarks/malaysia-ip-guide-2025); [Chambers: Trade Marks & Copyright 2026 – Malaysia](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/trade-marks-copyright-2026/malaysia/trends-and-developments)
- *Background knowledge, not verified this session:*
  - The Trademarks Act 2019 came into force on 27 December 2019, when Malaysia also joined the Madrid Protocol.
  - Copyright enforcement involves the Ministry of Domestic Trade and Cost of Living (KPDN) for criminal enforcement, alongside civil action in the High Court.
  - The Copyright (Amendment) Act 2022 added provisions mainly aimed at illicit streaming devices.

### Inferences
- In principle Malaysia follows the same idea/expression logic as other Berne/TRIPS jurisdictions. Copying code, art, audio or characters infringes; copying rules does not. With no local game-specific precedent, Malaysian courts would likely look to Commonwealth authority (UK, Singapore, Australia) on substantial similarity.
- The well-known-mark protection is the key point for this project. Supercell and Moonton can likely act in Malaysia against confusingly similar names or logos even without Malaysian registrations. MLBB is extremely popular in Malaysia, which strengthens a well-known-mark argument for "Mobile Legends".
- Practically, the main enforcement route against a small Malaysian studio is not a Malaysian lawsuit. It is store complaints (Google/Apple), which operate globally and fast, followed by a possible suit in a forum of the rights-holder's choosing.
- The studio should register its *own* title and logo with MyIPO, and via Madrid in key markets, before launch. That protects its brand and also surfaces conflicts early through clearance searches.

### Gaps
- No Malaysian court decision on video-game cloning or game mechanics was found.
- Exact section numbers of the Copyright Act 1987 (e.g., s.7 categories, s.36 infringement) and the Trademarks Act 2019 commencement date were not verified from the AGC Malaysia text, because fetches were blocked.
- No MyIPO guidance specific to games was found.
- Malaysia's Online Safety Act 2025 and its application to game platforms was not researched or verified.

## 5. Platform policies: Google Play and Apple App Store on impersonation and copycats

### Takeaway
Both stores ban copycats and impersonation and act on rights-holder complaints. Apple's Guideline 4.1 explicitly targets apps that copy other apps and (since a 2025 update) using another developer's brand, icon or product name in your app's name or icon without approval. Google's Impersonation policy bans misleading users about a connection to another developer or app. Enforcement is complaint-driven and inconsistent: clones are often removed only after the rights-holder complains, and Krafton sued Apple and Google in 2022 for *not* removing clones.

### Cited Findings
- **Apple Guideline 4.1 (Copycats).** App Review rejects apps that copy other apps and closes accounts that repeatedly submit copycat apps or attempt to impersonate a service. — [Apple App Review Help: 4.1 Prevent copycat rejection](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-review/guideline-reference/4-1-prevent-copycat-rejection)
- A later Apple update added that developers cannot use another developer's icon, brand or product name in their app's icon or name without approval. Coverage links this to a flood of Sora 2 clones in late 2025. Impersonation can also be a Developer Code of Conduct violation leading to removal from the Developer Program. — [9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/?p=1028296); [iDrop News](https://www.idropnews.com/?p=255514); [Conduct Atlas: Apple App copying and impersonation](https://conductatlas.com/platform/apple/apple-app-store-review-guidelines/provision/CA-P-018976/app-copying-and-impersonation-prohibited/)
- **Google Play Impersonation policy.** It prohibits developers from misleading users about an app's connection to someone else (another developer, company, entity) or another app. Enforcement can include policy strikes and removal under "Intellectual property and impersonation or deceptive behavior". — [Google Play Console Help: Impersonation](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9888374); [GoodBarber: Google IP infringement rejection](https://www.goodbarber.com/help/shop/resolve-google-play-store-rejections-r101/google-rejection-intellectual-property-infringement-a223/)
- Google has said apps must not have names or icons that appear "confusingly similar" to existing products. — [MCV](https://mcvuk.com/business-news/mobile/google-cracks-down-on-fake-apps/); [TechObserver: new Play Store policy to block copycat apps](https://techobserver.in/news/business/google-unveils-new-play-store-policy-to-block-copycat-apps-on-the-play-store-267619/)
- Google provides a reporting route for suspected copycats. — [Google Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16341334)
- **Enforcement inconsistency.** Krafton's January 2022 suit alleged Apple and Google were asked to stop distributing *Free Fire* and did not act. — [MacRumors, 13 Jan 2022](https://www.macrumors.com/2022/01/13/pubg-maker-sues-apple-over-clone-apps/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2022/01/13/pubg-mobile-maker-krafton-sues-apple-google-and-rival-game-developer-garena-over-clones/)
- **Precedent for removal.** *Mobile Legends: 5v5 MOBA* was pulled from Google Play after Riot complained and relaunched as *Mobile Legends: Bang Bang*. — [Sportskeeda](https://sportskeeda.com/esports/mobile-legends-tracing-origin-india-s-banned-game)

### Inferences
- Store policies are stricter than copyright law on **names, icons, screenshots and keywords**. A game can be legally non-infringing and still be rejected or removed for looking "confusingly similar". Store-listing assets (title, icon, screenshots, video, description, keyword field) are the highest-exposure surface.
- Avoid using "Clash Royale", "Mobile Legends", "MLBB", "Bang Bang" or "Supercell" in the title, subtitle, keyword field or description. Comparative phrasing such as "like Clash Royale" also risks impersonation and keyword-spam flags. Generic genre terms are safer.
- Once rival rights-holders complain, the store typically asks the developer to respond or resolve the dispute. Keeping a documented record of independent creation makes these disputes faster to answer.

### Gaps
- No quantitative data was found on how often clone games are removed after complaints, or on average time to removal.
- The current full text of Google's Impersonation and IP policies was not fetched, because fetching was blocked.

## 6. Loot box / gacha regulation: Malaysia, SEA, China, Korea, Europe, Australia, Brazil

### Takeaway
Probability disclosure is the global baseline. Apple has required it since December 2017 (Guideline 3.1.1) and Google Play since May 2019, and it is legally mandated in China (since 2017) and South Korea (statutory since 22 March 2024). Malaysia has no law classifying loot boxes as gambling. However, CMCF consulted (9 Sept to 24 Oct 2026) on a draft Gaming Sub-Code that would require disclosure of randomised mechanics and their odds, plus child protections, and a Federal Territories Mufti ruling treats paid loot boxes as impermissible. Australia requires at least an M rating for paid chance-based items (from 22 Sept 2024). Belgium treats paid loot boxes as illegal gambling (2018). Brazil banned loot boxes in games accessible to minors (in force 17 March 2026). The EU issued non-binding virtual-currency principles (21 March 2025), and a Digital Fairness Act proposal reportedly banning paid loot boxes for minors is due around November 2026.

### Cited Findings

**Platform rules**
- **Apple.** Since December 2017, App Store Guideline 3.1.1 states: "Apps offering 'loot boxes' or other mechanisms that provide randomized virtual items for purchase must disclose the odds of receiving each type of item to customers prior to purchase." — [Fenwick: Apple now requires disclosure of loot box odds](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/apple-now-requires-disclosure-of-loot-box-odds); [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/apple-now-requires-app-store-games-to-disclose-loot-box-odds/); [Conduct Atlas](https://conductatlas.com/platform/apple/apple-app-store-review-guidelines/provision/CA-P-018902/loot-box-apps-must-disclose-odds-before-purchase/)
- **Google Play.** Since May 2019, the Monetization and Ads policy states that apps offering mechanisms to receive randomized virtual items from a purchase ("loot boxes") must clearly disclose the odds in advance of purchase. Non-compliance risks removal. — [Fenwick: Google Play now requires disclosure of loot box odds](https://fenwick.com/insights/publications/google-play-now-requires-disclosure-of-loot-box-odds); [PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/google-play-developers-must-now-disclose-loot-box-odds)

Use direct-purchase cosmetics, a battle/season pass, and *earned-only* (not purchasable) chests or card drops. This sidesteps Belgium, Brazil, the reported Indonesia minor rule, the likely EU DFA minors ban, and Malaysian religious sensitivities, and avoids the Australian M-minimum.
- Because MLBB-style audiences in Malaysia, Indonesia and the Philippines skew young and Muslim-majority (Malaysia, Indonesia), paid gacha carries regulatory risk and also reputational risk with local communities.
- The Malaysian Sub-Code places obligations mainly on operators and platforms. A small Malaysian studio that self-publishes and runs its own servers is probably an "operator" for this purpose. This should be confirmed against the final text.

## 7. Practical do's and don'ts for this project (what to avoid copying; safe practices)

### Takeaway
Borrow freely at the level of *systems*: real-time deploy, a regenerating resource, a card deck, 3 lanes, towers, jungle, objectives. Make every *expressive* and *brand-facing* element original and visibly different: name, theme, art style, characters, map art, UI skin, icons, audio, card set and numbers. The case law (Tetris, Spry Fox vs DaVinci) and store enforcement (Mobile Legends 5v5's removal) both turn on surface similarity and confusion, not on mechanics.

### Cited Findings
- Re-skinning is not a defence when structure and presentation are copied wholesale. In Spry Fox, different art did not defeat a plausible claim where the hierarchy of objects and the antagonist creature were copied. — [MCV/Develop](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/court-denies-motion-to-dismiss-spry-fox-vs-6waves)
- Identical rules with a different theme and visual expression survived in DaVinci (Wild West vs Three Kingdoms). — [Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/texas/txsdce/4:2013cv03415/1134359/73/); [FKKS](https://fkks.com/news/how-courts-view-copyright-protection-for-video-games)
- Copying the visual "look and feel" of an identical-mechanic game infringed in Tetris; the court noted that the many alternative ways to express falling-block gameplay defeated merger. — [Wikipedia: Tetris v. Xio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetris_Holding,_LLC_v._Xio_Interactive,_Inc.)
- Trade secrets: Ironmace was liable for misusing information from an unreleased project developed at Nexon, even though copyright infringement was rejected (finalised 30 April 2026). — [Inven Global](https://www.invenglobal.com/articles/21463/nexon-wins-partial-supreme-court-victory-against-ironmace-in-trade-secret-misappropriation-case)
- Store rules prohibit using another developer's icon, brand or product name in your app name or icon without approval (Apple 4.1), and misleading users about a connection to another app (Google Impersonation). — [Apple App Review Help](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-review/guideline-reference/4-1-prevent-copycat-rejection); [Google Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9888374)
- Trade-press analysis suggests successful Clash Royale followers borrow progression systems rather than directly cloning core gameplay. — [PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/why-does-clash-royale-have-no-worthy-competition)
- Genre-defining words can be owned as trademarks (DOTA). — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2012-05-11-blizzard-and-valve-settle-dota-argument-blizzard-dota-is-now-bl.html)

### Inferences
These are concrete recommendations synthesised from the findings above. They are not lawyer-sourced verbatim guidance.

**DON'T**
- **Names and branding.**
  - Don't use "Clash", "Royale", "Legends", "Bang Bang", "MOBA Legends" or similar in the title or subtitle. Supercell holds CLASH registrations in Classes 9 and 41. "Mobile Legends" is likely a well-known mark in Malaysia and Indonesia.
  - Don't put competitor names in store keywords or descriptions, or "Clash Royale meets MLBB" in ad copy that appears in the store listing.
- **Resource and system names.** Don't call the deploy resource "Elixir" or show it as a pink/purple droplet bar. Don't call objectives "Lord" or "Turtle", or use "Lord"/"Baron"/"Dragon" equivalents with lookalike designs. Don't reuse rival terms like "Arena 1–N", "King Tower", "Princess Tower", "Crown Chest", or "Emblem"/"Battle Spell" naming.
- **Art and characters.** Don't make units or heroes recognisable as specific Clash Royale cards (e.g., a lumbering hog rider, a barbarian king with crown, a mini P.E.K.K.A-like armoured robot) or specific MLBB heroes. Don't copy silhouettes, colour schemes, or signature ability visuals and names.
- **Card set and numbers.** Don't replicate Clash Royale's card roster one-for-one, its exact costs (e.g., the 1–10 elixir scale with a 10-cap and 2.8-second regen), its stat tables, its 8-card deck/4-card hand structure combined with identical UI, or its exact chest timers and rarity tiers. A single shared convention is fine; replicating the whole *selection and arrangement* is what Spry Fox warns against.
- **Map.** Don't trace the MLBB/LoL map art, the exact placement of jungle camps and objectives, the river shape, or the base layout. Lanes, towers and a jungle are generic; a pixel-level lookalike map and minimap is expression.
- **UI and HUD.** Don't copy the Clash Royale bottom card tray and elixir-bar art, the arena layout art (two king towers plus four princess towers in the same positions and style), or MLBB's HUD skin (skill button arrangement art, minimap frame, shop UI). Function can be similar; skin and layout should differ.
- **Audio.** No sampled sounds or music, and no soundalike jingles, emotes or voice lines (e.g., laughing-king emote equivalents).
- **Assets and people.** Don't use ripped assets, asset-store packs that are themselves rips, or AI outputs prompted with "in the style of Clash Royale" or with MLBB hero names. Don't onboard ex-Supercell, Moonton, Tencent or Riot staff materials, design docs or data (trade-secret risk per Nexon v. Ironmace).
- **Monetisation.** Don't sell paid random chests or gacha without odds disclosure. Preferably don't sell paid random items at all if targeting minors, Belgium, Brazil, Indonesia, or the EU after the DFA.

**DO**
- **Theme.** Pick an original setting and theme that is clearly distinct from Clash's cartoon-medieval look and MLBB's fantasy-anime heroes (e.g., Nusantara or Southeast Asian mythology, sci-fi, or another distinct theme), as DaVinci's Wild West vs Three Kingdoms contrast illustrates.
- **Visual identity.** Build a distinctive visual identity: your own art bible, colour palette, character proportions, UI kit and iconography. Have the icon and screenshots reviewed side by side against both games before submission.
- **Systems.** Change the systems enough that the hybrid is genuinely new. For example, lanes with a different count or shape, a different resource model (shared lane energy, per-lane resources), hero plus card hybrids, and different win conditions. This reduces both legal risk and the "clone" perception that hurt Clash Royale imitators commercially.
- **Documentation.** Document independent creation: dated design docs, concept-art iterations, git history, art source files and contractor IP-assignment agreements. These make store disputes and any litigation answerable.
- **Clearance and registration.** Clear and register your own title and logo early with MyIPO, and via Madrid/WIPO in key markets such as Indonesia, the Philippines and the US. Run trademark searches for the title in Classes 9 and 41.
- **Contracts and licences.** Use written IP-assignment contracts with every employee, freelancer and outsourcer. Use licensed fonts, music and SFX, and keep the licences.
- **Monetisation design.** Design to the strictest common denominator:
  - published odds for any randomised items;
  - real-money price display for premium currency;
  - a single premium currency;
  - age-gating and parental controls;
  - direct-purchase cosmetics and battle pass rather than paid gacha.

  Track the final Malaysian Gaming Sub-Code and the EU DFA text before launch.
- **Patents.** Consider a basic freedom-to-operate check on high-risk areas. GREE's patents were asserted against Supercell's games (including a suit naming Clash Royale).
- **Legal review.** Get a short pre-launch review by a Malaysian IP lawyer, covering the store-listing assets especially.
